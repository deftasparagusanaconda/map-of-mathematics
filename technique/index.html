direct proof derives a conclusion straightforwardly from assumptions
proof by contradiction assumes the negation and derives an impossibility
proof by contrapositive proves an implication through its logically equivalent contrapositive
proof by cases covers every possible case separately
mathematical induction proves infinitely many statements from a base case and step
strong induction uses all earlier cases to establish the next one
infinite descent rules out solutions through an endless decreasing chain
diagonal argument constructs an object differing from every listed candidate
pigeonhole principle forces repetition when objects outnumber containers
double counting counts one collection in two different ways
extremal principle focuses on a maximal or minimal object
invariant principle tracks a quantity unchanged by allowed operations
monovariant tracks a quantity that moves only one way
probabilistic method proves existence by showing positive probability
averaging argument guarantees an example at least as good as an average
compactness argument derives global existence from finite consistency or local control
generating functions encodes sequences as formal power series
symmetry argument uses invariance under transformations to reduce complexity
colouring argument assigns labels or colours to expose combinatorial constraints
exchange argument proves optimality by locally replacing parts of a solution
greedy method builds solutions through locally best choices
divide and conquer splits a problem into smaller independent pieces
dynamic programming reuses solutions to overlapping subproblems
potential method tracks stored progress to bound amortised behaviour
